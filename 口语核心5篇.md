## 1. 地点 — Favorite City You Visited（大阪）

I'd like to talk about Osaka, hands down my favorite city. It's in the Kansai region of Japan — famous for street food, shopping districts, and a really vibrant vibe.

I first got curious about Osaka through those street food walking tours on Bilibili. After watching a bunch, I really wanted to see what daily life in a Japanese city actually feels like.

I went there in June 2025, right after graduation — kind of a celebration trip. It was a one-week Japan trip with a close friend. We landed in Osaka and spent the first few days there, staying near Shinsaibashi. Then we went to Kyoto for a bit, and took a night bus from Osaka all the way to the Tokyo area. But honestly, Osaka was the highlight of the whole trip.

What really struck me was how orderly everything was. The streets are spotless — yet there are almost no trash bins anywhere. The subway is so punctual it's almost funny. But it never felt dull. Walk around Shinsaibashi at night and it's just buzzing — neon signs everywhere, people lining up for takoyaki.

The most memorable spot was the Umeda Sky Building. I went up to the floating observatory — you're standing between two towers — and the night view was breathtaking. The whole city spread out beneath us, this endless grid of lights stretching to the horizon. That was the moment I realized Osaka was way bigger than I'd pictured.

So yeah, looking back, Osaka stuck with me not just because it was impressive — but because of what it meant at that time. It felt like a reward for four years of university. Like closing one chapter and starting another.

## 2. 人物 — Person Choosing a Tech Career（室友 Leo）

I'd like to talk about my college roommate, who has wanted to work in tech for as long as I've known him.

I met him in my first year of university when we were assigned to the same dorm room. Even back then, it was obvious he was headed for computer science. While the rest of us were figuring out how to skip morning classes, he was already up at six working through coding problems on LeetCode. He started thinking about this career in high school, actually — his uncle was a software engineer, and he once watched him build a simple inventory app for their family shop in a single weekend. He told me that seeing how a few hundred lines of code could solve a real problem — that was when he knew.

What he wants to do specifically is become an AI engineer. He's always been fascinated by how machines can learn — he says it's the closest thing to understanding how our own brains work.

The reason he chose this path, I think, goes beyond just liking tech. He genuinely wants to build things that help people. During university, he would spend weekends tutoring classmates who were struggling with programming — not for money, just because he liked seeing the moment when someone's code finally ran. I remember once I was completely lost in data structures, and he sat with me for three hours drawing tree diagrams until it clicked. That's the kind of person he is.

So yeah, watching him chase this has been pretty inspiring. It's rare to meet someone who found their calling that early and just... never wavered.

## 3. 经历 — Got Up Early（备考）

I'd like to talk about a time when I got up really early — and I mean painfully early, like 6 a.m.

This was about a month ago, right after I decided to take the IELTS. I had roughly two months to prepare, which sounds like a lot but really isn't when you're working on four skills at once. So I made this rule for myself: wake up at six every morning and do one hour of vocabulary before anything else.

Why did I get up early? Honestly, because there just weren't enough hours in the day. I work as a programmer — 10 to 7 every day — and by the time I get home and have dinner, my brain is basically useless. So mornings were the only time I could actually focus. I figured if I was serious about this exam, I had to steal the time from somewhere.

So every morning, the alarm would go off at six, I'd stumble out of bed, make a cup of tea, and sit at my desk with this vocabulary app. The first week was brutal — I'd just stare at words and nothing would go in. But after about ten days, something clicked. My brain started expecting it. I'd wake up without the alarm, which honestly shocked me.

How did I feel about it? At first, miserable. But looking back, it was probably the best decision I made during my whole prep. Those quiet early mornings became my most productive hours, and I actually started to enjoy them. Never thought I'd say that.

## 4. 物品 — Technology You Want to Own（iPad 7）

I'd like to talk about the iPad I bought recently, which is honestly one of the best purchases I've made.

It's an iPad 7 — not the latest model, which helped keep the cost down. But I had been thinking about it for a while, because I needed something portable for my IELTS preparation — mainly for reading PDFs and taking digital notes.

I first got to know about it through reviews on Bilibili. There are tons of videos comparing different iPad models, and I probably watched all of them. What sold me was seeing how people use it with a stylus — they'd annotate directly on practice tests, highlight passages, erase and rewrite. Compared to printing out stacks of paper every week, it looked so much more efficient.

Why would I like to own it — well, I already own it now, and I can confirm it's been incredibly useful. I use it for everything: reading practice passages, taking notes on vocabulary, doing writing tasks with split screen where the question is on one side and my essay on the other. It's also great for watching English videos without subtitles, which I do for listening practice.

It's not just a gadget, honestly. It's become my main study tool. I know some people buy iPads and then just use them for watching shows, but for me, it's genuinely transformed how I study. Best investment I made for this exam.

## 5. 志向 — Perfect Job（AI 安全研究员）

I'd like to talk about a perfect job I'd like to have in the future, which is to work as an AI security researcher.

I first got into this direction through my actual work. I'm currently a programmer, and my day-to-day involves building AI agents and analyzing malware samples. At first it was just a job. But the more I worked with it, the more I realized how fascinating this field is — you're basically studying how intelligent systems can be tricked, broken, or weaponized, and then figuring out how to defend against that. It's like a chess game where the rules keep changing.

To get this job at the level I want, I'd need to go further academically — probably a PhD. I'd need deeper knowledge in machine learning, specifically areas like clustering, anomaly detection, and adversarial robustness. A lot of the really interesting work in this field happens in research labs, not regular companies.

Why do I think it's a perfect fit? Two reasons. First, it's genuinely meaningful. As AI systems get deployed everywhere — finance, healthcare, autonomous driving — making sure they can't be compromised becomes a matter of public safety, not just tech curiosity. Second, it never gets boring. Attack methods evolve, defense methods evolve, and you're always learning something new. I'd much rather do that than a job where I'm doing the same thing every day.

So yeah, AI security researcher is the direction. I'm still early in the journey, but the path is pretty clear from here.
