## 1. Perfect Job（核心回答）

I'd like to talk about a perfect job I'd like to have in the future, which is to work as an AI security researcher.

I first got into this direction through my actual work. I'm currently a programmer, and my day-to-day involves building AI agents and analyzing malware samples. At first it was just a job. But the more I worked with it, the more I realized how fascinating this field is — you're basically studying how intelligent systems can be tricked, broken, or weaponized, and then figuring out how to defend against that. It's like a chess game where the rules keep changing.

To get this job at the level I want, I'd need to go further academically — probably a PhD. I'd need deeper knowledge in machine learning, specifically areas like clustering, anomaly detection, and adversarial robustness. A lot of the really interesting work in this field happens in research labs, not regular companies.

Why do I think it's a perfect fit? Two reasons. First, it's genuinely meaningful. As AI systems get deployed everywhere — finance, healthcare, autonomous driving — making sure they can't be compromised becomes a matter of public safety, not just tech curiosity. Second, it never gets boring. Attack methods evolve, defense methods evolve, and you're always learning something new. I'd much rather do that than a job where I'm doing the same thing every day.

So yeah, AI security researcher is the direction. I'm still early in the journey, but the path is pretty clear from here.

## 3. Movie You Watched and Enjoyed Recently

I'd like to talk about a movie I watched recently that I genuinely enjoyed — "The Imitation Game."

I watched it about two weeks ago at home on my iPad. I was taking a break from IELTS prep and wanted something that wasn't just mindless entertainment. A friend had recommended it to me ages ago, and I finally got around to it.

The movie is about Alan Turing, the British mathematician who cracked the German Enigma code during World War II. But it's not just a war movie — it's about this brilliant, socially awkward guy who basically invented the concept of modern computing while trying to save his country. The machine he built to decode messages was essentially the world's first computer.

What struck me most was how much he had to fight against everyone around him. His own colleagues thought his machine would never work. The military kept threatening to shut him down. But he just kept going, because he believed in the logic. And in the end, his work shortened the war by an estimated two years and saved millions of lives.

I chose this movie thinking it would be an interesting history lesson, but it ended up feeling surprisingly relevant. As someone studying CS, watching the story of how computing actually began was kind of inspiring. It made me appreciate that what we do with computers today stands on the shoulders of people like Turing. Also, Benedict Cumberbatch was incredible in it.

## 7. New Law You Would Like to Introduce

I'd like to talk about a new law I'd like to introduce in my country, which would require mandatory security testing for AI systems before they're deployed in high-risk areas.

The law would require that any AI system used in critical infrastructure — things like power grids, financial systems, autonomous vehicles, medical devices — must pass a standardized security audit before going live. The audit would specifically test for vulnerabilities like adversarial attacks, data poisoning, and agent manipulation — the kinds of things I work on in my day job.

What changes would this bring? First, it would force companies to take AI security seriously from the start, not as an afterthought. Right now, a lot of AI systems are built for performance first and security is barely considered. Second, it would create a clear standard — what does "secure enough" actually mean? Without a law, it's just whatever each company decides.

Would it be popular? Tech companies would probably push back because it adds cost and slows down deployment. But I think the public would support it once they understood what's at stake. We've already seen cases where AI systems were manipulated in ways their creators never anticipated.

I came up with this idea through my own work in AI agent development and malware analysis. Every day I see how relatively easy it is to find weaknesses in these systems. A law requiring security audits wouldn't solve everything, but it would be a baseline — a minimum bar that everyone has to clear.

## 8. Law on Environmental Protection 🆕

I'd like to talk about an environmental protection law that I think is really important — China's mandatory waste sorting policy that was rolled out in major cities a few years ago.

I first learned about it when it came to Shanghai in 2019. It was all over the news and social media — people joking about having to figure out whether their leftover soup counted as "wet waste" or "residual waste." It became kind of a national conversation.

Who benefits from it? Honestly, everyone. In the short term, it reduces the amount of waste that ends up in landfills and makes recycling more efficient. In the long term, it helps reduce pollution and conserves resources. But I think there's a less obvious benefit too — it forces people to think about their waste. Before this law, most people never gave a second thought to where their trash went. Now, you have to actively decide what goes where. That small act of sorting is a daily reminder that your consumption has consequences.

How do I feel about this law? I actually think it's one of the most practical environmental policies I've seen. My roommate Leo — who's big on environmental stuff — got really into it. He set up a sorting system in our dorm and even made these little cheat-sheet cards for everyone on our floor. What I like about the law is that it doesn't just punish — it educates. Over time, people stop seeing sorting as a burden and start seeing it as just... normal. That's how real change happens.

## 9. Story/Book with Animals 🆕

I'd like to talk about a book with animals in it that I read when I was younger — "Watership Down."

It's about a group of rabbits who have to leave their warren and find a new home. But it's not really a children's book — it's quite dark and complex. The rabbits have their own language, their own mythology, even their own political systems. The main characters — Hazel, Fiver, Bigwig — are rabbits, but they feel completely human in their personalities and struggles.

I read it in high school, actually in English. It was one of the first full English novels I ever finished, so it holds a special place for me. At the time, I chose it because the cover looked interesting — just a rabbit silhouette against a sunset. I had no idea it was going to be this epic adventure about leadership, survival, and community.

What do I think of it? It's one of those books that stays with you. On the surface, it's a story about rabbits looking for a new home. But underneath, it's about the cost of leadership, the tension between safety and freedom, and how different societies organize themselves. The fact that all the characters are animals somehow makes the themes hit harder — you're not distracted by human politics or cultural references.

I think stories with animals often work that way. By making the characters not human, you actually get closer to universal human truths. The rabbits of Watership Down taught me more about courage than most human characters I've read.

## 12. Food People Eat on Special Occasions

I'd like to talk about dumplings, which are probably the most important festival food in China, especially during Spring Festival.

Spring Festival is the biggest holiday of the year — it's like Christmas and New Year combined. Families gather from all over the country, and on New Year's Eve, everyone sits around the table and makes dumplings together. It's not just about the food — it's a whole family activity.

How they're made is pretty straightforward but takes practice. You make the dough from flour and water, roll it into small circles, then fill each one with a mixture of minced pork, cabbage, ginger, and soy sauce. Then you fold the wrapper over and pinch the edges to seal it. The folding is the hard part — getting the shape right and making sure they don't burst open when you boil them. My grandmother can fold one in about three seconds. Mine look like flattened footballs.

Why do people eat them on this occasion? There are a few layers. The shape of a dumpling looks like an ancient Chinese gold ingot, so eating them symbolizes wealth and prosperity for the new year. But I think the deeper reason is that making dumplings is something you do together. It takes time, so everyone sits, talks, laughs, and works. In a world where we're all busy and scattered, dumplings are an excuse to be in the same room for a few hours. That's probably more meaningful than any symbolic meaning.

## 13. Important River/Lake 🆕

→ **归入地点组**，用道顿堀运河（见 `口语素材-地点组-大阪.md`）。以下为备用国内素材：

I'd like to talk about the Yangtze River, which is the longest river in China and honestly one of the most important rivers in the world.

It stretches over 6,300 kilometers, cutting right through the heart of China from the Tibetan Plateau all the way to Shanghai. It's not just big — it's central to almost everything. The Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze is the largest hydroelectric power station on the planet. The river provides water for hundreds of millions of people. It's been a transportation route for thousands of years.

I learned about it in school, of course, but I actually saw it for the first time a few years ago when I visited a city along the river with my family. It was wider than I'd imagined — almost like looking at the sea in some sections. And the amount of cargo ships going up and down was incredible. It really is like the highway of China.

Why is it important? Well, economically, it's hard to overstate — shipping, irrigation, power, fishing. Culturally, it's been a source of inspiration for poets and artists for millennia. And environmentally, it's both a vital ecosystem and a major environmental challenge. Protecting the Yangtze is basically protecting a huge part of China's future. That's why there are now strict laws about pollution and fishing along the river.
