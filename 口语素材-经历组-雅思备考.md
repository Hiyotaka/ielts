# 口语素材 — 经历组：雅思备考

## 1. Got Up Early（核心回答）

I'd like to talk about a time when I got up really early — and I mean painfully early, like 6 a.m.

This was about a month ago, right after I decided to take the IELTS. I had roughly two months to prepare, which sounds like a lot but really isn't when you're working on four skills at once. So I made this rule for myself: wake up at six every morning and do one hour of vocabulary before anything else.

Why did I get up early? Honestly, because there just weren't enough hours in the day. I work as a programmer — 10 to 7 every day — and by the time I get home and have dinner, my brain is basically useless. So mornings were the only time I could actually focus. I figured if I was serious about this exam, I had to steal the time from somewhere.

So every morning, the alarm would go off at six, I'd stumble out of bed, make a cup of tea, and sit at my desk with this vocabulary app. The first week was brutal — I'd just stare at words and nothing would go in. But after about ten days, something clicked. My brain started expecting it. I'd wake up without the alarm, which honestly shocked me.

How did I feel about it? At first, miserable. But looking back, it was probably the best decision I made during my whole prep. Those quiet early mornings became my most productive hours, and I actually started to enjoy them. Never thought I'd say that.

## 2. Plan You Had to Change Recently

I'd like to talk about a plan I had to change recently, which was honestly pretty frustrating at the time.

This happened about three weeks ago. I had mapped out this really detailed study schedule for the IELTS — two hours of listening on Monday, reading practice on Tuesday, writing on Wednesday, speaking on Thursday, and so on. I was following it religiously for about a week and it was going great.

Then my company suddenly gave me an urgent task — I had to audit a batch of AI agent skills for our OpenCLAW platform. It came out of nowhere, right in the middle of my prep period. I had to work overtime for several days straight. No IELTS. No practice. Nothing. So my carefully designed study plan completely fell apart.

The new plan was way less ideal. Instead of hitting all four skills evenly, I had to prioritize — so I cut speaking and writing that week and just focused on reading and listening during whatever free time I could find, even if it was just 30 minutes on my lunch break.

How did I feel? Honestly, I was pretty annoyed. I had been in a good rhythm, and losing that momentum really bothered me. But I also realized something useful — plans are going to break. The point isn't to have a perfect plan. It's to be flexible enough to adjust when things go wrong, and not use one disruption as an excuse to give up entirely.

## 3. Worked in a Group

I'd like to talk about a time when I worked in a group, which was during my IELTS preparation last month.

I had been studying alone for about two weeks, and I was hitting a wall, especially with speaking and writing. You just can't practice those properly by yourself. So I found this small study group online — there were four of us, all taking the test around the same time.

What we did was pretty simple. Every evening at 8 p.m., we'd hop on a voice call. Two nights a week we'd do speaking practice — one person would play the examiner, ask Part 2 and Part 3 questions, and the other person would answer while the rest listened and gave feedback. One night a week we'd exchange writing — we'd each write a Task 2 essay, drop it in the group chat, and everyone would comment on each other's work. The other nights were just casual check-ins.

The main problem we faced was that everyone was at different levels. One guy was already scoring like 7.5 in reading while I was still struggling to hit 7. So sometimes the feedback felt either too advanced or too basic. We solved it by pairing up — the stronger reading person would help others with reading, and I'd help with, well, moral support mostly.

I joined the group because honestly, IELTS prep is incredibly lonely. Staring at practice tests alone in your room for weeks is draining. Studying with others made it feel less like a chore and more like a shared goal. And I improved way faster with their feedback than I ever would have alone.

## 4. Important Decision You Made

I'd like to talk about an important decision I made recently, which was to cut down on overtime and side projects so I could focus on preparing for the IELTS.

I made this decision about two months ago. I was looking at my calendar and realizing that between my full-time job — I work as a programmer, 10 to 7 — and occasional overtime, I had maybe an hour a day for IELTS prep. That's nowhere near enough when your target is 6.5 or above. Something had to give.

How did I make the decision? I basically listed out all my commitments and asked myself: what's more urgent right now? The IELTS has a deadline — May 24th. If I don't get the score I need, I'd have to wait months for the next available test date. Some of the extra work projects could wait. So the math was pretty clear.

The result was that I talked to my team leader and explained my situation. He was surprisingly understanding — told me to prioritize the exam for now and shift some less urgent tasks to after my test date. That freed up about two extra hours each evening, which I put entirely into writing and speaking practice.

Why was it important? Because for the first time, I was making a deliberate trade-off instead of trying to do everything and doing nothing well. I think that's a pretty adult decision — recognizing that you can't have it all and choosing what matters most right now.

## 5. Live Sports Event You Watched

I'd like to talk about a live sports event I watched recently, which was actually a nice break during my IELTS prep.

It was an NBA playoff game — I don't remember the exact teams, but it was a really close game that went down to the last minute. I watched it at home on my iPad, sometime in early May. I was supposed to be doing a reading practice test that evening, but I was feeling completely burnt out, so I decided to give myself a break.

I watched it by myself in my room, which is actually how I prefer to watch sports — I know some people like watching with friends, but I get too distracted. I had snacks, I had the volume up, and for two hours I completely forgot about IELTS.

Why did I like it? Honestly, it wasn't just about the game itself. It was the fact that I let myself enjoy it without guilt. During intense prep periods, every minute you're not studying feels like you're wasting time. But that evening I realized that's not true — taking a real break actually made me more focused the next day. I woke up and did a reading test and scored higher than I had all week.

So yeah, it was just a basketball game, but it taught me something about balance. You can't sprint for two months straight. Sometimes you need to just watch a game and not think about anything else.

## 6. Not Allowed to Use Your Mobile Phone

I'd like to talk about an occasion when I wasn't allowed to use my phone — which was during a mock IELTS test about two weeks ago.

I had signed up for this practice test at a local test center — you know, one of those full simulations that mimics the real exam conditions. They were very strict about the rules. Before entering the test room, the invigilator asked everyone to hand over their phones. Not just turn them off — physically hand them over. They put them in a sealed bag with our names on it, and we couldn't get them back until the entire test was over, which took about three hours.

At first, I felt kind of naked without my phone. Like, I kept reaching into my pocket out of habit and finding nothing there. It was a weirdly uncomfortable feeling, which made me realize how attached I am to this device.

But here's the thing — once the test started, I completely forgot about my phone. I was too focused on the listening section and trying to catch every word. Three hours flew by, and when I finally got my phone back, I had like 50 unread messages — and none of them were urgent. It made me realize that most of the time, we check our phones not because we need to, but just out of habit.

So actually, being separated from my phone was kind of freeing. I've tried to keep that habit on my own study days now — phone in another room, no notifications. My focus is way better.

## 7. Gave Advice to Others

I'd like to talk about a time when I gave advice to someone, which happened just last week with a colleague of mine.

He saw me coming out of the library with a stack of IELTS practice books and asked if I was preparing for it too. Turned out he was thinking about taking the test but had no idea where to start. He said he'd been watching random YouTube videos but felt completely overwhelmed.

So I told him three things. First, don't start with random videos — start with one real Cambridge practice test to figure out your baseline. You can't make a plan until you know where you are. Second, put 80% of your energy into reading and listening, because those are the fastest to improve for Chinese students. Writing and speaking take way longer to move the needle. And third, for speaking, don't prepare 50 separate answers — just prepare a few flexible stories and learn how to adapt them to different questions.

I gave him this advice because I had wasted a lot of time at the beginning myself. I had spent two weeks just watching videos and scrolling through tips, without actually doing a single practice test. I didn't want him to make the same mistake.

He actually took the advice — he messaged me a few days later saying he'd done a practice test and figured out his weak points. It felt good to be useful. When you're in the middle of something yourself, helping someone else reminds you how far you've come.

## 8. Many People Were Smiling / Happy Event

I'd like to talk about an occasion when many people were smiling, which happened during an online speaking practice session with my study group.

This was about two weeks ago. There were five of us on the call. One guy — let's call him Xiao Wang — was practicing a Part 2 topic. The topic was "Describe a food that people eat on special occasions." And he was supposed to talk about dumplings during Spring Festival. Pretty standard.

But somewhere in the middle, he mixed up his words and accidentally said that his family eats "children" during Spring Festival instead of "dumplings." There was this two-second silence, and then everyone just burst out laughing — including him. The correct word was jiǎozi, and he had somehow merged it with háizi. His face turned bright red, but he was laughing the hardest.

The whole call just became really light after that. We were all so stressed about our own mistakes, and seeing someone mess up in such a ridiculous way and just laugh about it made us all relax a bit. We ended up spending the last 20 minutes sharing our funniest language mistakes. Turns out everyone has a collection of embarrassing moments when learning a language.

Most people were smiling because it was just genuine — nobody was making fun of him, we were all just relieved that we weren't the only ones making stupid mistakes. That's one thing I really like about the language learning community. Everyone's been there.

## 9. Felt Proud of a Family Member / Proud Moment

I'd like to talk about a time I felt genuinely proud — and it was actually about myself, which feels a bit weird to say, but I think it counts.

This was about three weeks into my IELTS preparation. I had been grinding through reading tests, and my scores were stuck at around 6.5. Reading is supposed to be my strongest skill, so that was honestly pretty demoralizing. I kept making the same types of mistakes — mostly True/False/Not Given questions where I'd overthink and talk myself out of the right answer.

Then one evening, I sat down to do a full reading test — passage 1, 2, and 3, under timed conditions. And when I checked my answers, I had scored a 7.5. It was the highest I'd ever gotten. I actually checked the answer key twice because I didn't believe it.

The thing I was proud of wasn't the score itself — it was that I had kept going even when I was stuck. For three weeks, every practice test looked the same, and it would have been so easy to convince myself I just wasn't improving. But I didn't stop. I kept making mistake after mistake, and eventually, something shifted. Not dramatically — just a small jump from 6.5 to 7.5. But that jump meant the work was paying off.

I called my mom that night and told her. She probably didn't fully understand what a 7.5 on IELTS reading meant, but she was happy because I was happy. That was a good moment.

## 10. Didn't Enjoy the Music Played

I'd like to talk about an event where I didn't enjoy the music, which was at this café near my office that I sometimes go to after work.

I went there about a month ago, alone, planning to do a full writing practice session — Task 1 and Task 2 back to back, which takes about an hour. I picked this café specifically because it's usually quiet and has good Wi-Fi.

But that day, they had some kind of new playlist on — this really loud electronic music with a heavy bass. I don't know how to describe it exactly, but imagine trying to write an essay about environmental policy while someone is thumping a beat directly into your brain. It was impossible to focus.

I decided to go there anyway because I had already ordered my coffee and found a seat, and honestly, finding another spot near the office at that time of day is a nightmare. So I just put on my own headphones and played some white noise to drown it out. It kind of worked, but the bass still leaked through.

Why didn't I enjoy it? Well, I'm not against electronic music in general — at a club or a party, fine. But in a café where people are clearly working and studying, it just felt like the wrong music in the wrong place. I think venues should think about what their customers are actually doing there, not just what's trendy.

## 11. Time You Needed to Use Your Imagination

I'd like to talk about a time I needed to use my imagination, which was during a writing practice session for IELTS.

This was about two weeks ago. I was practicing Task 2, and the essay question was something like: "Some people believe that governments should spend more money on public transportation rather than roads. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"

The problem was, I needed concrete examples to support my argument, but I didn't have any real data in my head. I couldn't exactly Google statistics in the exam, right? So I had to use my imagination.

I basically created a fake city in my head — I called it "City X" — and invented a scenario. I wrote something like, "For example, a medium-sized city that invests in a light rail system could see a 30% reduction in traffic congestion within five years." Was that number real? Absolutely not. But it felt plausible, and it served the argument.

How difficult was it? At first, it felt really unnatural. In school, we're always told to use real examples and cite real data. But in IELTS, nobody's fact-checking you. The examiner cares about your language, not whether your statistics are accurate. Once I understood that, using my imagination became almost fun. It's like being a novelist — you create a believable world just long enough to make your point, and then you move on.

I felt pretty liberated afterwards. It's one less thing to worry about in an already stressful exam.

## 12. Encouraged Someone to Do Something

I'd like to talk about a time when I encouraged someone to do something they didn't want to do.

The person was a colleague of mine, and what I encouraged him to do was sign up for the IELTS. He'd been talking about studying abroad for like two years, but he kept putting off actually taking the test. Every time I brought it up, he'd say, "Yeah, I'll do it next month." Next month became six months.

This was about three weeks ago, when I was in the middle of my own prep. I told him, look, the test isn't going to get any easier, and the registration fee isn't going to get any cheaper. Actually, that's not true — I told him the fee had just dropped to 1990 RMB, so actually it was getting cheaper. But my point was that the perfect time to start never comes. You just have to pick a date and commit.

How did he react? At first, he was defensive — the usual excuses about being too busy, needing more time to prepare. But I think what changed his mind was when I showed him my practice test scores. Not my best ones — my worst ones. The 5.5 in writing. The speaking practice recording where I stuttered through half the answers. I wanted him to see that everyone starts somewhere, and waiting until you feel ready means waiting forever.

He registered for the test last week. I don't know if he'll get the score he wants, but at least he's no longer stuck in the "maybe next month" loop. And honestly, encouraging him made me take my own prep more seriously. You can't tell someone else to stop procrastinating and then go procrastinate yourself.

## 13. Challenging Technological Problem 🆕

I'd like to talk about a technological problem I faced recently that was surprisingly difficult to solve.

The problem was with the IELTS registration website. I was trying to book my test date about a month ago, and the website just kept crashing. I'd fill out all my information — name, ID number, test center preference — hit submit, and the page would just go blank. I tried it on three different browsers, on my laptop, on my phone. Same result.

This was honestly pretty stressful because test spots in my city fill up fast, especially for the speaking test slots. The later you register, the worse time slots you get. So every hour the website wasn't working was an hour where my preferred date might get fully booked.

What made it challenging wasn't the technical difficulty — it was that I couldn't figure out if the problem was on my end or theirs. I cleared my cache, switched networks, restarted my computer. Nothing worked. Finally, I went on Weibo and searched for "IELTS registration website not working," and found dozens of other people complaining about the same thing. So the website itself was just broken.

The solution was frustratingly simple: wait. I tried again at 2 a.m., when presumably fewer people were on the website, and it finally went through. I paid, got my confirmation, and immediately went to bed. The whole experience reminded me that sometimes the solution to a tech problem is just patience, not skill.

## 14. Problem with an Electronic Device 🆕

I'd like to talk about a problem I had with an electronic device, which was my headphones dying right before a really important study session.

This was about two weeks ago, during my IELTS prep. I was about to do a full listening practice test — all four sections, 30 minutes, the whole thing. Listening is my weakest skill, so these practice sessions were crucial for me. I sat down at my desk, opened the practice test audio file, put on my wireless earbuds, and one side was completely dead. No sound at all.

I tried the usual fixes — put them back in the case, reset the Bluetooth connection, even cleaned the little metal contacts with a cotton swab. Nothing. The right earbud was just gone.

What made this especially frustrating was the timing. It was late evening, around 9 p.m., so I couldn't exactly go out and buy new ones. And you can't do a listening test on laptop speakers — the audio quality isn't good enough, and in the real test you have headphones.

So in the end, I borrowed a pair of wired earphones from my roommate. Old school, with a cable and everything. And honestly? They worked perfectly. No battery, no Bluetooth pairing, just plug in and go. I did the listening test, scored a 6.5 — which was actually pretty good for me at the time.

It was a small problem, but it made me realize how dependent I'd become on wireless everything. Sometimes the simplest solution is the one that works best.

## 15. Message/Email Took Long to Reply 🆕

I'd like to talk about a time when I received a message and it took me a really long time to reply.

The message was from a friend who had taken the IELTS last year and scored an 8. He had offered to share some of his preparation materials with me — practice tests, vocabulary lists, speaking templates. Really generous offer. He sent me a long WeChat message detailing everything he could send over.

And I just... didn't reply. Not because I didn't want to — I really did. But I kept overthinking my response. I wanted to sound grateful without being overly formal, professional without being cold. It was such a nice gesture that a simple "thanks" felt insufficient, but writing a long heartfelt message felt weird since we're not that close.

So I kept putting it off. One day became three days. Three days became almost a week. I'd see the message notification, feel a wave of guilt, and swipe it away. Every day that passed made it harder to reply because now I'd have to apologize for the delay on top of everything else.

Finally, I just forced myself to write a straightforward reply — thanked him sincerely, asked for the materials, and briefly apologized for the late response without making a big deal out of it. He replied within minutes with a link to the files, completely unfazed by my delay.

I felt pretty stupid afterwards. I had spent a week stressing about the perfect reply, and in the end, a simple one was all that was needed. It taught me that overthinking a message is almost always worse than just sending an imperfect one.

## 16. Sent a Message But Received No Reply 🆕

I'd like to talk about a time when I sent a message to someone and got no response, which was honestly a bit awkward.

I had sent a message to a former manager I respected, asking if he could be a reference for a graduate school application. I spent a long time crafting the message — explained my situation, reminded him which project we had worked on together, expressed how much I valued his mentorship. It was a well-written message, I thought.

I sent it on a Monday morning. By Wednesday evening, still nothing. By Friday, I had given up and started thinking about who else I could ask. The thing is, on WeChat, you can see when someone has read your message if they have read receipts on. And he had read it. So I knew he saw it and chose not to respond. That somehow felt worse than if he'd just not seen it.

I did eventually get a reply — almost two weeks later. He apologized and said he'd been buried in a product launch and my message had slipped his mind. He did end up writing the reference, so it all worked out.

But those two weeks of waiting were genuinely uncomfortable. I kept oscillating between "he's just busy" and "he's avoiding saying no." The experience taught me that when someone doesn't reply, it's almost never personal. People have their own chaos going on. But I also told myself that if anyone ever sends me a similar message in the future, I'll at least reply with "let me think about it" so they're not left hanging.

## 17. Organized a Happy Event Successfully 🆕

I'd like to talk about an event I helped organize that turned out really well — a group mock speaking test for about ten students.

This was about two weeks ago, in the middle of my IELTS prep. A bunch of us in the study group were feeling anxious about the speaking section — you can practice reading and listening alone, but speaking is different. So I thought, why not organize a proper mock test where everyone gets a real practice run?

I prepared for it over about three days. First, I booked a meeting room at my office that we could use on a Saturday afternoon — asked my team leader for permission, and he was fine with it. Then I downloaded the latest speaking question bank and printed out cue cards for Part 2. For Part 1 and Part 3, I compiled a list of common questions. I also made a schedule — each person would get about 15 minutes total, and the rest of us would be the audience and give feedback.

My study group helped a lot — one person handled the timing, another person set up a phone to record each speaker so they could review their own performance later. On the day itself, about ten people came, which was more than I expected.

Why was it successful? Not because it was perfectly organized — it wasn't. But because everyone left feeling a little less scared of the speaking test. After being the "examiner" for a few rounds, you realize the exam isn't as intimidating as it seems. Several people messaged me afterwards saying they felt much more confident. That was the goal, so I'd call that a win.

## 18. Special Day Out That Cost Little Money 🆕

I'd like to talk about a special day out that cost almost nothing, which happened just a couple of weeks ago.

It was a Sunday, and I was feeling completely drained from studying. I needed to get out of my room, but I was on a tight budget — most of my money had gone to the IELTS registration fee, which was 1990 RMB. So whatever I did, it had to be cheap.

I decided to go to the city library — the big public one downtown that I'd always meant to visit but never had. The bus fare was two yuan. The library is free to enter. I spent the entire morning there, just browsing. I wasn't even looking for anything specific. I drifted through the history section, flipped through some photography books, read a couple of short stories. No agenda, no pressure.

Around noon, I bought a sandwich from a convenience store — maybe 15 yuan — and ate it in a small park next to the library. The weather was perfect, sunny but not hot. I sat on a bench and watched people walk their dogs and kids run around. It was completely unremarkable and exactly what I needed.

The whole day cost me less than 20 yuan, but it felt genuinely special. I think during intense periods of preparation, you forget that not everything has to be productive. Doing nothing, in a nice place, by yourself — that has value too. I went home feeling refreshed, and the next morning my study session was probably twice as effective.

## 19. Stuck in a Traffic Jam 🆕

I'd like to talk about a time when I was stuck in a really bad traffic jam — which was actually on my way to a mock test, which made it especially stressful.

This was about three weeks ago. I had signed up for a practice IELTS test at a test center across town. The test was supposed to start at 9 a.m. sharp, and they were very clear that latecomers wouldn't be admitted. So I left home at 7:30, thinking an hour and a half would be plenty of time.

About 20 minutes into the journey, the traffic just stopped. Completely. We were on this main road and nothing was moving. At first I thought it was just a normal rush-hour slowdown, but after 15 minutes of barely crawling forward, I realized it was serious. Apparently there had been an accident further up the road.

What did I do while waiting? Stressed, mostly. I kept checking the time on my phone every two minutes, recalculating whether I'd make it. I pulled out my vocabulary app and tried to review some words, but I couldn't concentrate at all. The minutes were ticking by and I was imagining myself arriving at the test center and being turned away at the door.

We eventually started moving again around 8:40. I got to the test center at 8:55 — five minutes to spare. I literally ran from the bus stop to the building. I made it, but the whole first section of the listening test, my heart was still pounding from the stress of almost being late.

It taught me a lesson — always leave way more buffer time than you think you need, especially for anything exam-related. The stress of almost missing it was almost worse than the test itself.
