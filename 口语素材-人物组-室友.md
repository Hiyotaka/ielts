## 1. Person Choosing a Tech Career（核心回答）

I'd like to talk about my college roommate, Leo, who has wanted to work in tech for as long as I've known him.

I met him in my first year of university when we were assigned to the same dorm room. Even back then, it was obvious he was headed for computer science. While the rest of us were figuring out how to skip morning classes, he was already up at six working through coding problems on LeetCode. He started thinking about this career in high school, actually — his uncle was a software engineer, and Leo once watched him build a simple inventory app for their family shop in a single weekend. He told me that seeing how a few hundred lines of code could solve a real problem — that was when he knew.

What he wants to do specifically is become an AI engineer. He's always been fascinated by how machines can learn — he says it's the closest thing to understanding how our own brains work.

The reason he chose this path, I think, goes beyond just liking tech. He genuinely wants to build things that help people. During university, he would spend weekends tutoring classmates who were struggling with programming — not for money, just because he liked seeing the moment when someone's code finally ran. I remember once I was completely lost in data structures, and he sat with me for three hours drawing tree diagrams until it clicked. That's the kind of person he is.

So yeah, watching Leo chase this has been pretty inspiring. It's rare to meet someone who found their calling that early and just... never wavered.

## 2. Person with Successful Business

I'd like to talk about Leo again — my college roommate. Besides being a CS student, he also started his own online education business, which I think is pretty impressive.

I got to know him in our first year of university when we shared a dorm. Around our third year, he noticed that a lot of students in our program were struggling with the same subjects — data structures, algorithms, operating systems — and the tutoring center on campus was expensive and always booked. So he thought, why not build something cheaper and more accessible?

He spent about six months building this mini program on WeChat where students could book one-on-one tutoring sessions with senior students. He did all the coding himself. He started with just five tutors from our department. Within a few months, it had about a dozen tutors covering the core CS courses.

What makes the business successful, in my opinion, isn't the tech — it's the pricing. He charges about half of what the campus center charges, and part of the fee goes directly to the tutors. So students get affordable help, and tutors actually want to join. It's simple but it works.

I think what I admire most is that he built this while still doing CS coursework full-time. The guy just doesn't sleep, apparently.

## 3. Person Who Plans a Lot

I'd like to talk about Leo, my college roommate, who is honestly the most organized person I've ever met. The guy plans everything.

I noticed this about him from our first week of university. Every Sunday evening, he sits down with his laptop and plans out the entire week — and I mean every hour. Classes, study blocks, meals, gym, even "free time" is scheduled. At first I thought it was kind of ridiculous, like, who schedules free time? But after a while, I realized he was getting twice as much done as the rest of us.

What kinds of plans does he make? Well, there's the weekly schedule I mentioned. But he also has a semester plan for his grades, a monthly budget, and even a five-year career roadmap. He showed it to me once — it had milestones like "get a FAANG internship," "contribute to an open-source project," "launch my own product." This was in our second year. Most of us didn't even know what we were having for dinner.

The thing is, his plans actually work. He's hit almost every milestone on that roadmap. I used to think planning was a waste of time — you know, life is unpredictable, why bother? But watching Leo taught me that planning isn't about predicting the future. It's about knowing what matters to you and making sure you don't drift away from it.

So yeah, I find him pretty impressive. Not because he's naturally gifted, but because he's intentional about everything he does.

## 4. Person Good at Learning Languages

I'd like to talk about Leo, my roommate, who has this almost annoying ability to pick up languages.

I first realized this when I overheard him on a phone call speaking Japanese. I knew he was Chinese, obviously, and his English was already really good — we used English in some of our courses. But Japanese? I had no idea. Later he told me he taught himself Japanese in high school just by watching anime and using language apps. No formal classes, no tutor. Just... did it.

The way he learns is actually pretty systematic. For Japanese, he started with the basics using Duolingo and Anki for vocabulary, then moved on to watching Japanese dramas with subtitles, then without subtitles, and eventually started reading Japanese news articles out loud to practice pronunciation. For English, he did something similar — he told me he used to watch Friends episodes on repeat, first with Chinese subtitles, then English, then none. He'd shadow the actors' lines until his accent matched theirs.

Currently he speaks Chinese natively and has solid English from his CS courses. He's been self-studying Japanese for about a year — still not fluent, but he can hold a basic conversation, which I think is pretty impressive for someone learning on their own.

What I find most admirable is that he never studied languages for a test or a certificate. He just genuinely enjoys the process. He once told me that learning a language is like getting a key to a whole new world — you don't just learn words, you learn how other people think. That stuck with me.

## 5. Person Who Often Helps Others

I'd like to talk about Leo, who is honestly one of the most helpful people I know.

He helps others pretty much constantly — it's not a once-in-a-while thing. During university, he ran free tutoring sessions every weekend for classmates who were struggling. He built that whole education mini program just to make tutoring more accessible. And when COVID hit and everything went online, he spent hours helping professors figure out how to use Zoom and set up their virtual classrooms.

Why does he do it? I've asked him this before, and his answer was surprisingly simple. He said that when he was young, his family didn't have much money, and a lot of people helped him along the way — teachers who stayed after class, neighbors who shared textbooks, strangers who gave him scholarships. He said he's just paying it back.

But I think it goes deeper than that. He seems to genuinely enjoy seeing other people succeed. Like, when a student he tutored got an A on an exam, he'd be happier than if he got the A himself. It's almost weird how much satisfaction he gets from other people's wins.

Honestly, knowing him has made me reflect on myself a bit. I used to think helping others was something you did when it was convenient. But Leo does it like it's just... part of being alive. It's kind of changed my perspective on things.

## 6. Person Looks After the Natural World

I'd like to talk about Leo, who is surprisingly passionate about the environment — surprising because he's a CS student, not an environmental science major.

He does a few things regularly. Every other weekend, he volunteers with a local environmental group — they do river cleanups, tree planting, that kind of thing. He also runs a small WeChat group where he shares tips about reducing plastic waste and recycling. And in our dorm, he was the one who set up a system to separate our trash properly, which took some getting used to, honestly.

He also got a few friends from our department involved — he'd post in the class group chat about local cleanup events and occasionally a handful of people would show up.

I think what drives him is a kind of quiet sense of responsibility. He once told me that as someone who'll spend his career building technology, he feels responsible for the waste it might generate — like, if you're creating the future, you should also clean up after yourself. That logic is pretty hard to argue with.

I feel genuinely impressed by him. A lot of people talk about caring for the environment, but he actually goes out and does something about it, every single week.

## 7. Person Who Solved a Problem in a Smart Way

I'd like to talk about Leo again, and a problem he solved that I thought was pretty clever.

The problem was our department's course scheduling. Every semester, about 200 students needed to sign up for elective courses, and the process was a mess — it was done through a clunky university website that would crash constantly. Students would spend hours refreshing the page, and some would still miss out on the courses they needed.

Leo got frustrated with this — he was one of the students refreshing at 6am — and decided to fix it. He built this simple WeChat mini program that pulled the course data from the university system and displayed it in a clean, filterable list. You could search by time slot, by professor, by course type — things the official system didn't support. And it sent you a notification when a spot opened up in a full course.

The smart part wasn't the tech itself — it was that he didn't try to rebuild the whole system. He just built a better front end on top of what already existed. It took him maybe two weeks. And the university IT department never complained because he wasn't modifying any data — just displaying it in a more useful way.

Within a month, basically everyone in our department was using it. I think what made it smart was that he saw a problem, understood exactly what would fix it, and didn't overcomplicate things. Most people would have just complained about the system. He actually did something.

## 8. Person Helped You Solve a Problem 🆕

I'd like to talk about a time when Leo helped me out of a pretty stressful situation.

The problem was that I was failing data structures in my second year. It wasn't that I wasn't trying — I was spending hours re-reading the textbook, copying code from the slides. But nothing was clicking. The exam was three weeks away and I was honestly panicking.

Leo noticed I was stressed and just asked, "You want some help?" No judgment, no lecture. He sat down with me and asked me to explain a concept back to him. After about five minutes, he stopped me and said, "You're memorizing the code, not understanding the logic." And he was right. I could write a binary tree traversal from memory but I couldn't explain why it worked.

So he changed my entire approach. Instead of copying code, he made me draw out the algorithms on a whiteboard and trace through them step by step. Then he'd ask, "What happens if we change this condition? What if the tree is unbalanced?" — forcing me to think about the underlying logic rather than just the syntax. We did this for about an hour every evening for two weeks.

I ended up passing that exam — not just passing, I got a solid B. But more than the grade, I learned how to actually learn. That approach — understanding the why, not just the what — I still use it today.

I felt incredibly grateful. He didn't have to spend his evenings helping me, but he did, and he never made me feel stupid for not getting it.

## 9. Person Who Loves to Grow Vegetables

I'd like to talk about Leo, my roommate, who has a pretty unexpected hobby — he grows vegetables on our dorm balcony.

It started in our second year. He ordered some pots and soil online, and before I knew it, there were cherry tomatoes, lettuce, and these little chili peppers growing outside our window. In a dorm room. On the sixth floor.

He takes it pretty seriously. Every morning, first thing after waking up, he checks on the plants — waters them, turns the pots so they get even sunlight, picks off any dead leaves. He even talks to them, which I used to make fun of. He uses an app to track when each plant was last watered and fertilized.

Why does he do it? He told me it started because he missed his grandmother's garden back home. Growing up, she had this huge vegetable patch, and he spent a lot of time helping her. So the balcony garden is kind of a connection to that memory. But he also says there's something deeply satisfying about eating something you grew yourself. He once made us a salad entirely from balcony vegetables. I have to admit, those tomatoes were incredible.

I think it's pretty cool, honestly. In the middle of a concrete city, on a tiny sixth-floor balcony, he created this little green space. It's not practical in any real way — you can't feed yourself from six pots — but I think that's not the point. The point is the act of growing something.

## 10. Friend from Childhood

I'd like to talk about Leo, who is a close friend — we actually met in university, but it feels like I've known him since childhood.

We met on the first day of university when we were assigned to the same dorm room. I remember walking in and seeing this guy already unpacked, desk organized, a schedule pinned to the wall. I thought, okay, this is going to be an interesting four years.

What we often did together was study, mostly — but not in the boring way. We had this routine where we'd go to the library at 8pm, study for two hours, then go to the noodle shop across the street and talk about everything except school. Movies, life, random philosophy questions, his latest vegetable-growing experiment. Those late-night noodle sessions are honestly some of my best memories from university.

What made me like him was his energy. He's one of those people who makes you want to be better — not by lecturing you, but just by being himself. When you see someone wake up at six, study hard, help others, learn languages, start a business — all without complaining — it kind of rubs off on you. I started waking up earlier just because he did. I started planning my week because his planning seemed to work so well.

Even though we only met in university, the friendship we built feels deeper than a lot of childhood friendships I've seen. Sometimes you meet someone and it just clicks, and it doesn't really matter how long you've known them.

## 11. Famous Person You Would Like to Meet

I'd like to talk about someone I'd really like to meet — Kai-Fu Lee, who's one of the most well-known figures in the Chinese tech industry.

I first learned about him through Leo, my roommate. Leo had his book "AI Superpowers" on his desk, and one day I picked it up and couldn't put it down. Kai-Fu Lee worked at Apple, Microsoft, and Google before founding Sinovation Ventures, but what really got me was his perspective on how AI will reshape how we live and work.

I would want to meet him in a casual setting — maybe over coffee, not at some conference. Because what I'd really want to ask him isn't about his career moves or AI predictions. I'd want to ask him how he stays optimistic about technology while being honest about the jobs it might replace. His book talks about how AI could free people to do more creative, human work — but I wonder how realistic that really is for most people.

I'd like to meet him because he's one of the few tech leaders who seems genuinely thoughtful about the human side of technology. A lot of people in tech just chase the next big thing, but he's always asking what the cost is. That kind of balanced thinking is rare, and I'd love to hear it from him directly.

## 12. Child Who Loves Drawing

I'd like to talk about Leo's younger cousin, who I met once when he visited our campus. The kid is about eight years old and absolutely obsessed with drawing.

I first met him when Leo brought him to our dorm one weekend. The kid walked in, saw our whiteboard — which we used for algorithm diagrams — and immediately asked if he could draw on it. Within ten minutes, the entire board was covered in spaceships, dinosaurs, and this elaborate underwater city with fish wearing hats. I was honestly kind of amazed.

Leo told me he draws constantly — at home, at school, on restaurant napkins, basically anywhere there's a surface. His parents bought him a tablet for digital drawing last year, and now he spends hours on it every day after finishing his homework.

Why does he love it so much? From what I observed, I think it's because drawing is how he processes the world. Like, when he saw something interesting, he didn't just talk about it — he had to draw it. That spaceship came from a science video he watched. The underwater city was from a trip to the aquarium. Drawing seems to be his way of understanding things, not just a hobby.

It made me think about how most of us lose that as we grow up. Kids just create because they want to — there's no self-consciousness, no worrying about whether it's good enough. I think adults could learn something from that.

## 13. Person Who Works in a Successful Company

I'd like to talk about a former classmate who now works at Tencent — I got to know about his career through Leo.

This guy, his name is Zhang Wei, was a senior in our department. He studied computer science and was one of those people who was always building something — apps, games, little tools. Leo knew him because they collaborated on the tutoring mini program, and Zhang Wei helped with some of the backend code.

What he does at Tencent is work on their cloud computing platform — specifically, he helps design the systems that handle huge amounts of data for other companies. It's pretty technical stuff, way over my head. But from what Leo told me, he started as a regular engineer and worked his way up to a team lead position in about three years.

I think Tencent is a successful company for obvious reasons — it's one of the biggest tech companies in the world, and its products like WeChat are used by literally everyone in China. But what I find interesting is that Zhang Wei chose to stay there even though he had offers from smaller startups that probably would have paid more. He told Leo that at Tencent, the scale of the problems is just different — you're building systems that have to work for hundreds of millions of people. That kind of challenge is hard to find elsewhere.

I find it pretty motivating, honestly. It shows that if you're really good at something, there are places where you can work on problems that actually matter at scale.
